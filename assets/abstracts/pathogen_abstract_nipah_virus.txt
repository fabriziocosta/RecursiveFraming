TITLE

Evidence of Nipah Virus Spillover from Fruit Bats to Humans Associated with Contaminated Date-Palm Sap Consumption

BACKGROUND

Nipah virus is a zoonotic paramyxovirus maintained in populations of fruit bats of the genus Pteropus. Human infection has been associated with direct contact with infected animals, exposure to contaminated food, and subsequent person-to-person transmission. The ecological and clinical signals that distinguish an isolated spillover event from an outbreak with sustained human transmission remain incompletely characterized.

METHODS

We investigated a cluster of acute febrile encephalitis cases occurring in a rural agricultural community in Bangladesh during the winter date-palm sap harvesting season. Patients were identified through hospital-based surveillance and were classified according to a standardized clinical case definition. Serum, throat-swab, urine, and cerebrospinal-fluid specimens were tested for Nipah virus RNA by reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction and for anti-Nipah immunoglobulin M by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. A case-control investigation assessed consumption of raw date-palm sap, contact with fruit bats or livestock, and exposure to symptomatic household members. Environmental sampling was performed at date-palm collection sites, and bat specimens were collected under appropriate biosafety and wildlife protocols.

RESULTS

Nipah virus infection was confirmed in patients with fever, altered mental status, and rapidly progressive encephalitis. Most confirmed patients reported consuming raw date-palm sap collected from trees bearing fresh evidence of fruit-bat feeding. Viral RNA was detected in residual sap and on protective coverings installed over collection vessels, and Nipah virus–reactive antibodies were detected in sampled Pteropus bats from the surrounding area. The association between raw sap consumption and confirmed infection remained strong after adjustment for age, occupation, household crowding, and contact with livestock. A smaller number of secondary cases occurred among household contacts of symptomatic patients; these cases were associated with prolonged exposure to respiratory secretions and direct care during the encephalitic phase of illness. Viral sequences from human and environmental specimens clustered closely with one another and were consistent with a common local transmission source.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings support a zoonotic spillover pathway in which Pteropus fruit bats contaminate raw date-palm sap, followed by infection of humans who consume the untreated product. Limited person-to-person transmission occurred after the initial animal-to-human jump but did not produce evidence of sustained community transmission. Covering collection vessels, avoiding consumption of raw date-palm sap, strengthening clinical surveillance for acute encephalitis, and implementing rapid infection-control measures may reduce the risk of recurrent Nipah virus emergence.
